# Split Decision: Prototype Playtest Guide

## Purpose

Use this protocol to determine whether the ordering context helps Grade 6 learners
reason about grouping and repeated fees without the interaction or story doing the
mathematical thinking for them.

## Participants

- Primary: students ages 11–12 with basic experience evaluating expressions
- Secondary: mathematics educators or learning designers reviewing the prototype
- Suggested first round: 3–5 learners in individual sessions

## Session length

Approximately 20–30 minutes: 3 minutes of orientation, 12–18 minutes using the
prototype, and 5–8 minutes of follow-up questions.

## Facilitator opening

Say: “This is an early prototype, so I am testing the experience—not you. Please
say what you notice, expect, and find confusing. I may stay quiet while you work.”

Do not explain the delivery-fee structure or the meaning of the parentheses before
the learner encounters them.

## Observation prompts

- What does the learner predict before testing either checkout?
- Do they recognize that a fee belongs to each active order?
- Can they connect each visual arrangement to its expression?
- When expanding `2(x + 3)`, do they repeat both terms?
- Does moving the pizza-price control help them notice the constant difference?
- Do they understand the purpose of the counterexample level?
- Can they transfer the structure to tickets without returning to the pizza model?
- Where do they hesitate, reread, or click without a clear prediction?

## Follow-up questions

1. Why did the two checkouts have different totals?
2. What does each part of `2(x + 3)` mean in the pizza situation?
3. How is `2x + 3` different?
4. Would changing the pizza price remove the difference between the checkouts? Why?
5. How could you convince someone that two expressions are not equivalent?
6. What felt satisfying, surprising, or confusing?

## Evidence of success

- The learner predicts or explains that two separate orders repeat the fee.
- The learner interprets parentheses as defining the repeated order, not as a
  symbol-processing rule only.
- The learner explains `2(x + 3) = 2x + 6` using the quantities in two receipts.
- The learner uses a counterexample appropriately.
- The learner recognizes the same structure in the ticket-fee transfer problem.

## Revision triggers

- If learners cannot identify what repeats, strengthen the visual attachment
  between each fee and its order.
- If learners guess expressions without referencing the model, ask for a prediction
  before displaying answer choices.
- If learners treat the slider as decoration, make them record two cases and compare
  the differences explicitly.
- If the final transfer feels like a new problem type, add a brief bridge asking
  what corresponds to the pizza price, order, and fee.

## Data to retain

Capture completion time, first-choice reasoning, incorrect-answer patterns,
facilitator notes, and the learner's final verbal explanation. Avoid treating
completion alone as evidence that the learning goal was met.
