# Split Decision

**Same items. Different total. Why?**

**Live prototype:** `PASTE_SPLIT_DECISION_DEMO_URL_HERE`  
**Repository:** `PASTE_SPLIT_DECISION_REPOSITORY_URL_HERE`

## Overview

Split Decision is an independent interactive mathematics-learning prototype for all-age beginners working at approximately Grade 6. It uses a familiar online-order context to help learners understand grouping, equivalent expressions, and the distributive property.

Two pizzas can be placed in one combined order or split across two separate orders. A $3 delivery fee attaches to every active order. Reorganizing the same pizzas changes which costs repeat, making the difference between \(2(x+3)\) and \(2x+3\) visible and meaningful.

## Central question

> How can the same two items have different totals?

## Learning goals

Learners are invited to:

- Connect grouping with a recognizable unit: one complete order
- Interpret \(2(x+3)\) as two orders that each contain an \(x\)-dollar pizza and a $3 fee
- Interpret \(2x+3\) as two pizzas sharing one delivery fee
- Explain why \(2(x+3)=2x+6\)
- Explore an invariant by changing the value of \(x\)
- Understand why one matching input does not prove equivalence
- Transfer the structure to ticket prices and per-ticket booking fees

## Standards alignment

The prototype is primarily aligned with:

- CCSS 6.EE.A.2: Write, read, and evaluate expressions
- CCSS 6.EE.A.3: Apply properties of operations to generate equivalent expressions
- CCSS 6.EE.A.4: Identify when two expressions are equivalent

## Prototype structure

The seven-level vertical slice progresses through:

1. Comparing one combined order with two separate orders
2. Representing two separate orders with \(2(x+3)\)
3. Representing one combined order with \(2x+3\)
4. Expanding two receipts into \(2x+6\)
5. Varying the pizza price to identify a constant difference
6. Finding a counterexample to a false equivalence claim
7. Transferring the structure to ticket booking fees

## Design focus

This prototype was created to explore:

- Familiar context as a mathematical model rather than a decorative story
- Action before formal notation
- Multiple representations of the same cost structure
- Immediate, misconception-specific feedback
- Variable manipulation and invariant noticing
- Structural explanations after numerical testing
- Responsive, all-age visual design
